# Daily brief

Researched 2026-09-16 from Becky Root's account of writing for the President's
Daily Brief (The Cipher Brief) and Aaron Berman's guide to writing for busy
decision-makers, both people who did this for a living. Rules only, each one
falsifiable. Vibes were left out on purpose.

**One page. Hard stop.** "PDBs are short products, typically just one page."
The constraint is the craft: analysts "strive to provide the most information
they can, in the least amount of real estate on a page." A brief that scrolls
is a report wearing a brief's name.

**The first sentence carries the whole thing.** Bottom line up front means the
what, the why and what happens next land "within the first sentence of your
document, not buried later." Berman sizes it at two to three lines containing
"ALL the information needed for a busy reader to understand the key point." If
the reader stops after sentence one, they should still have been briefed.

**Answer four questions, in this order.** Root gives the skeleton as what is
happening now, why it is occurring, what has changed, and what the possible
next steps are. The third one is what most briefs drop, and it is also the only
reason to send a brief daily rather than once.

**Do not show your homework.** "Your client is paying for your analysis, they
do not need to see all of your homework." Every line of raw material that made
the judgment possible and is not the judgment gets cut.

**Title in five to seven words.** Berman's limit. A title that needs a colon
and a subtitle is two titles.

**Ban the hedge words.** "Probably, likely, may, could" are called out by name
as ambiguous. The replacement is not a fake number: "focus on the RISK and what
to do about it." Say what is at stake and what to do, and the uncertainty
becomes actionable instead of decorative.

**No value-laden language, no subjective judgment.** Same source. The brief
reports and analyses. It does not editorialise, and a reader who can feel the
writer's opinion stops trusting the facts next to it.

**Say exactly what you mean, and use small words.** Berman states both halves
together: "Say exactly what you mean. EXACTLY. But also, don't use big words."
Precision and plain vocabulary are not in tension.

**Bullets, not paragraphs.** "Use bullets, instead of in a paragraph. It's
easier to read that way." Inverted pyramid throughout: main point, then
supporting arguments, then evidence.

**Declare an information cut-off.** Root: "identify the most important
information for your client and develop an information cut-off time." A brief
without one cannot be trusted on what it omits, because the reader cannot tell
absence from staleness.

**Typos cost credibility, disproportionately.** "Even minor grammatical errors
and formatting mistakes can put your credibility at risk." The reader cannot
audit the analysis, so they audit what they can see.

## When the brief contradicts these

The usual ask is "include everything from today" or "add a section for each
source." That is the homework, and it is the named failure. Say which rule it
breaks, in one line. Build the one-page version. Offer the full dump as a
separate artifact if it is still wanted. Silently delivering either one is
wrong.
