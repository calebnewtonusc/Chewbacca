# Application essay

Researched 2026-09-16 from a former Dartmouth admissions officer's account of
reading the pile, College Essay Guy and Collegewise on show-don't-tell, plus one
outcome retro that diffed five rejections against two advances in a single
application cycle. Rules only, each one falsifiable. Vibes were left out on
purpose.

**Assume the reader has your resume open.** The named failure is "The Walking
Résumé": "Your Activities List already covers what you've done. Don't use the
essay to rehash it." Anything the form already knows is spent words.

**A credential is the most information per word and the least persuasion per
word.** From the cycle retro: prompts with 250 words to spend opened on a
person and advanced. Prompts with 75 to 200 words got a claim plus credentials,
because credentials compress well, and all of those were rejected. A short
answer has room for one concrete thing and no credentials.

**Show the evidence and let the reader reach the verdict.** "When you tell, you
skip the evidence and jump to the verdict. When you show, you present the
evidence and let the reader deliver the verdict themselves." Naming your own
trait does the reader's job for them and they will not accept it.

**Start in the middle.** Drop the reader _in medias res_ rather than warming
them up. The named failure is "The Five-Paragraph Journey to Nowhere," and the
specific tell is filler stage direction: "I stood up, walked down the hall."
Stories need a beginning, not an introduction.

**Hyper-specific detail is the only thing that cannot be duplicated.** The
defence against a generic essay is "a draft full of hyper-specific details that
only you could know," meaning names, numbers and sensory particulars. Test
every sentence by asking whether another applicant could submit it unchanged.

**Abstract virtue nouns are the giveaway.** "Passion this and excellence that.
Passion and excellence are those telling kind of words: they're general,
abstract nouns that sound pretty and often mean little."

**Write in the register you actually speak in.** "Admissions readers, who read
hundreds of essays a season, can tell instantly when a sentence isn't in a
student's natural register." Reaching for a bigger word is legible as reaching.

**Hardship is not a thesis.** Two of the five named bad categories are about
this. "Oversharing is not the same as being reflective," and "a tough moment
doesn't automatically make for a strong essay." Difficulty earns nothing on its
own; what you did with it might.

**Do not claim you fixed anything.** "The Savior Complex": "You didn't fix
global poverty during your weeklong service trip." Scale the claim to what
actually happened.

**Answer every clause of the prompt.** A prompt asking two things and getting
one answered reads as a prepared essay pasted in. This is the most common
mechanical cause of rejection and the easiest to check.

**Nothing on the page you cannot say out loud, cold.** From the same retro: an
essay strong enough to advance put its author in an interview he could not hold
up in, because the writing had gone somewhere the memory had not. If a line
cannot be spoken from memory under pressure, it is a liability rather than an
asset.

## When the brief contradicts these

The usual ask is "make it sound impressive" or "work in the internship and the
award." That is the walking résumé, by name. Say which rule it breaks, in one
line. Build the version with one concrete thing in it. Silently delivering
either one is wrong.
