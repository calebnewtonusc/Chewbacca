# Demo video

Researched 2026-09-16 from two teardowns by the co-founder of HowdyGo, who has
"made and reviewed thousands of SaaS product demos", plus Consensus' five-example
piece. Rules only. Vibes were left out on purpose.

**One to three features, never more.** Named outright as "the absolute biggest
mistake that I see people make when creating product demo videos... otherwise
people will just switch off." This is the rule the person asking will override.
"Show all the screens" is this mistake with a friendly name.

**A storyline, not a tour.** Slack's demo is praised for "a clear narrative and
a story line to this whole demo... they're not jumping around between different
aspects of the product." Ask what single job the viewer watches somebody finish.

**The product usually wrote its own storyboard.** Look at the onboarding or
how-it-works copy. Silo's says "Three taps to dinner: browse live drops, claim
your spot, show the QR at pickup." That is the demo, written by the founder.

**Interrogate every beat.** "Go through and go: is this actually adding value,
is this really giving people an idea of what the product is capable of." On
Slack's demo he cuts the emoji reaction: "you can already kind of tell that from
the UI."

**Cut the login.** "I often see people record their login process, but it's not
really something that people find that interesting. So you might as well cut it
out."

**Show it with data in it.** "One of the beautiful things is that you get to
show your product with a lot of data in it, because if you have a free trial
it's not the same." Empty states are not a feature. A populated product is the
entire advantage a demo has over a trial.

**Pre-fill anything the viewer would have to type.** On the Como demo: "you
don't have to fill in any of your details because it's already pre-filled...
how many times have you wanted to try something out and you get to the point
where it's like plug in your email details."

**Do not over-zoom.** "Don't add too many zooms. It can actually be a little bit
dizzying... most people really want to see the context about what you're
interacting with." Zoom for one specific thing, then pull back out. Exception is
a vertical cut for LinkedIn, TikTok or YouTube.

**Increase complexity over time.** The Gemini demo is praised for it: a plain
opener, then roughly a minute of escalating capability. "They're not just diving
straight into it."

**Describe the benefit, not the action.** "Don't just describe exactly what's
happening on screen. Instead, try to lean towards describing the benefits that
the user actually gets from doing those actions." Not "now we tap the filter":
"you only see what is still open near you."

**End on a CTA.** "The people who reach this point in your product demo are so
engaged... it's worth having a CTA because they can dive straight into their
product, sign up for an account and away you go." Two is normal: a primary
matching the demo, plus a lower-commitment one.

**Do not record your voice while capturing.** "Go back and insert your voice
recording later. It'll just make it easier to clip and trim."

**Pair every feature with its benefit, and keep the UI interactions fast.** From
the Salesforce1 teardown: fast interactions communicate ease of use without the
narration having to claim it.

**Real customers beat a polished narrator** where you have them. Vivint anchors
its demo in actual customers using the system: social proof, a face for the
persona, and it doubles as an informal sales team.

**Complexity is not an excuse.** A Siemens turbine and a Norwegian national
virtual power plant both demo fine on video. The benchmark offered: after
watching, could you explain it to somebody with no background in the field?

## When the brief contradicts these

Say which rule it breaks, in one line. Build the narrative version. Offer the
literal version as a separate artifact if it is still wanted. Silently
delivering either one is wrong.
