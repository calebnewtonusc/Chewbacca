# Study guide

Researched 2026-09-16 from Roediger and Karpicke's "Retrieval-Based Learning: A
Decade of Progress" (ERIC ED599273), which reviews roughly ten years of the
experiments, plus the 2006 and 2007 Purdue Learning Lab papers it draws on.
Rules only, each one falsifiable. Vibes were left out on purpose.

**Make them retrieve, never re-read.** This is the whole finding. Roediger and
Karpicke 2006: students who read a passage and then tried to recall it forgot
13% over two days, while students who re-read it forgot 56%. A guide that
presents material back to the learner is the 56% condition with nicer styling.

**Free recall beats multiple choice, whatever the exam looks like.** "Initial
recall tests tend to promote greater gains in learning than initial recognition
tests do, regardless of the format of the final assessment (recall or
recognition)." The reason given is effort: "recall tests provide less support
and require greater retrieval effort." Do not soften a question into options
because options are easier to grade.

**A multiple-choice question without feedback actively plants wrong answers.**
The lures are the danger: learners are "exposed to incorrect information (the
lures on the test) that they might retain in the long term," and on a later
cued-recall test "students tended to produce the incorrect responses that they
had selected on the initial multiple-choice test." The fix is stated plainly:
"it is possible to reduce or eliminate the negative effects of multiple-choice
questioning by providing feedback." So multiple choice is allowed only when the
guide shows the correct answer afterwards. No exceptions.

**Do not drill the same item repeatedly in one sitting.** "Massed repeated
retrieval produced no benefit relative to dropping items after one recall."
Once they get it, move on. A guide that asks the same thing three times in a
row is spending the learner's attention for nothing.

**Space the repeats evenly. Do not bother with expanding intervals.** The older
advice was to expand the gaps (0-1-5-9). The review's verdict: "equally spaced
schedules produce retention that is the same as or sometimes better than
retention produced by expanding schedules." Both beat massed practice, which is
the comparison that actually matters. Even spacing is also simpler to build, so
there is no reason to do the complicated thing.

**Pitch the difficulty so they can actually retrieve.** Retrieval only helps
when it succeeds, and the paper's own methodological advice is to "increase
retrieval success by providing more initial retrieval support or by giving
initial tests very shortly after material has been studied." A question nobody
can answer teaches nothing. First pass should be answerable; later passes get
harder.

**Retrieval beats every fancier study activity tested against it.** Including
the one that looks most like real studying: "retrieval practice produces more
learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping." If a guide feature
competes for time with answering questions, the questions win.

**Make the guide remember what they missed.** The entire value of a second
session is spending it on the items that failed, and that requires recording
per-item results rather than a single score. A guide that only reports "7/10"
cannot schedule anything.

## When the brief contradicts these

The usual ask is "summarise the chapter" or "make me a review sheet," which is
the re-reading condition. Say which rule it breaks, in one line. Build the
retrieval version. Offer the summary as a separate artifact if it is still
wanted. Silently delivering either one is wrong.
