# Retrieval practice, spacing, and cards

## Why questions and not summaries

Retrieving a fact strengthens the path to it. Reading the fact again does not,
much. This is the most replicated result in the learning literature and it is
also the least followed, because retrieval is unpleasant and rereading is easy.

Practical consequence: every artifact you produce for the user should be
something that asks them a question, not something that tells them an answer.

## Writing a good question

- **One idea per question.** Two ideas means a partial answer you cannot score.
- **Ask for the mechanism, not the label.** "What happens to the membrane when
  cholesterol increases at low temperature" beats "what is fluidity".
- **Ask across the material, not down it.** The questions that catch real gaps
  compare two things from different lectures.
- **Include application items.** A case not in the lecture, where the concept has
  to be run rather than recited.
- **Write the answer at the same time**, with a source. An unscoreable question
  is a waste of the attempt it costs.

## Grading an attempt

Score against the source and be specific about what was missing. "You have the
mechanism and the wrong direction" is actionable. "Close" is not. When an answer
is right for the wrong reason, say so, because it will fail differently next
time.

Then reask the missed items later in the same session, and again in the next.

## Flashcards

Cards are for atomic material: terms, values, structures, pairs. Do not make
cards for processes; make questions for processes.

Tab-separated is the format every tool imports, Anki included. One card per
line, question tab answer:

```
What does the fluid mosaic model describe?	The structure of the cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer with proteins moving laterally through it
Chapters covered by Midterm 1	1 through 5
```

Rules that keep a deck useful:

- **Minimum information principle.** A card that lists four things is four cards.
- **No cards from a source you have not read.** A wrong card drills an error in.
- **Cloze deletions for sequences.** Hide one step, keep the rest as context.
- **Tag by lecture or chapter**, so a deck can be filtered to an exam's range.

## Spacing

Gaps are the mechanism. The specific intervals matter far less than that the
gaps exist at all.

A workable default when an exam is two weeks out: day 1, day 2, day 4, day 7,
day 11, day 14. Shift it earlier for material that was shaky in the diagnostic
and later for material that was solid.

For weekly quizzes with a fixed window, the honest schedule is a short retrieval
pass the day the material is taught and a second one the day before the quiz
closes. Two ten-minute passes beat one forty-minute pass and take less total
time, which is the part that makes it stick as a habit.

## Interleaving

Studying topic A until it is solid, then B, then C produces better practice
performance and worse test performance than mixing them. The mixing forces the
retrieval of _which_ approach applies, which is exactly what an exam asks and
blocked practice never trains.

So: mixed problem sets, questions in random order, and topics revisited out of
sequence. Expect it to feel worse. That feeling is the effect working.
