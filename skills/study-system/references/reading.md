# Assigned reading

## Read with the question in hand

Reading to have read it and reading to answer something are different
activities, and only the second one survives the week. Before opening the text,
find out what it is for: a graded response, a discussion, a problem set, an exam
chapter. That decides how to read it.

If the assignment prompt exists, read the prompt first. A reading response with
five specific questions turns the reading into a search, which is both faster and
better.

## A textbook chapter for an exam

1. **Skim the structure first**: headings, bold terms, figures, the summary at
   the end. Five minutes, and it makes the read itself land somewhere.
2. **Read a section, then close it and say what it said.** This is the whole
   technique. The part that cannot be said is the part that was not read.
3. **Convert as you go**, into questions rather than highlights. Highlighting is
   rereading with a pen.
4. **Figures are content**, not decoration, in a science text. Many exam
   questions come straight off them.

## A paper

Read it in passes, not once carefully:

- **Pass 1:** title, abstract, figures, conclusion. Now you know the claim.
- **Pass 2:** intro and results, checking whether the figures support the claim.
- **Pass 3:** methods, only if the result matters enough to check how it was
  made.

Most papers deserve pass 1. Some deserve pass 2. Reading everything at pass 3 is
how a reading list stops getting done.

## Reading for a graded response

The response usually wants four things, and they are usually stated in the
prompt:

- The central claim, in your own words. Not a quote.
- The reasoning or evidence behind it, and whether it holds.
- Where it connects to what the class has been arguing about.
- Your critique: what is missing, what is assumed, who would disagree.

Annotate for those four while reading, not after. Going back through a text to
find a quote is the slowest part of writing the response, and it is avoidable by
marking the page number when the sentence is in front of you.

## Length requirements are a floor with a purpose

A 350-word response that answers the four questions beats a 700-word one that
restates the article. Word counts exist because they are the length at which the
questions can actually be answered, so hitting the count with summary is missing
the point rather than gaming it.

## Under time pressure

When the reading will not get done in full, do it in this order: the prompt, the
abstract or intro, the conclusion, the figures, then the middle if time remains.
Say what was skipped rather than writing around the gap. Instructors recognize a
response built from the abstract, and a narrower argument that is actually
supported reads better than a broad one that is not.
